# Amherst Public Health: Ticks

Source: https://www.amherstma.gov/3784/Ticks (captured 2026-10-24)

Ticks
WHY SHOULD YOU CARE ABOUT TICKS?

In regions like New England, where ticks are prevalent, it's essential to understand the importance of taking precautions against them. Ticks are notorious for transmitting diseases such as Lyme disease, babesiosis, anaplasmosis, Powassan virus and alpha-gal syndrome, among others. These illnesses can cause a range of symptoms, from mild discomfort to severe neurological complications and even death. Lyme disease, carried by Black-legged deer ticks (see below), is most common here in the Northeast. In 2025, more than a quarter of the cases in the region were in Amherst residents. This may be due to the fact that many Amherst residents like to spend time outdoors and the recreational areas in our town tend to have more tallgrasses than other local ecosystems.

While ticks are most active during warmer month’s (April-September), tick exposure can happen year-round as long as temperatures are above freezing. Ticks prefer to live in grassy, brushy, wooded areas when they are not attached to a living host. Activities that put you at risk for tick exposure include spending time outside in your yard, gardening, walking your dog, camping, hunting and hiking - among other outdoor activities. Be mindful that pets that go outdoors may carry ticks inside with them when they return home. 

COMMON TICKS IN MASSACHUSETTS

The two most common types of ticks found in Massachusetts are the Black-legged ticks (lxodes scapularis), also called the deer tick and the Dog Tick (Dermacentor variabillis). Recently the Lone Star Tick (Amblyomma americanum) has been identified on the Cape and Islands. 

An emerging tick-borne disease, alpha-gal syndrome, is receiving more attention in Massachusetts these days. Alpha-gal syndrome (AGS) is a serious, potentially life-threatening allergy to alpha-gal (a molecule that is naturally produced in most mammals but not in people) that can develop after a tick bite. When a tick bites, it can transfer alpha-gal from its saliva into a person’s blood.

AGS symptoms occur after people who have been bitten eat red meat or consume other products made from mammals. Symptoms can occur 2-6 hours after consuming products containing alpha-gal, like red meat or dairy products, and can include hives, rashes, stomach pain, nausea, vomiting and diarrhea.
In the U.S, the lone star tick is the species most associated with alpha-gal syndrome, however other ticks may also trigger it. Lone star ticks are usually found in the Southern and South-Central states, but they are becoming more prevalent in MA, especially on the Cape and Martha’s Vineyard. There have been recent reports of lone star ticks in Berkshire County, so this is now becoming a local concern. The Massachusetts Department of Public Health recently added alpha-gal syndrome to its list of reportable diseases.
PREVENTION TIPS
Stick to the center of trails when hiking!
Perform tick checks: After spending time outdoors, conduct thorough tick checks on yourself, your children, and your pets. Pay close attention to areas such as the scalp, behind the ears, underarms, groin, and behind the knees. Ticks can be as small as a poppy seed, so use a mirror or ask someone to help with hard-to-see areas.
Wear protective clothing: When spending time outdoors, especially in wooded or grassy areas where ticks thrive, wear long-sleeved shirts, long pants tucked into socks, and closed-toe shoes. Light-colored clothing can make it easier to spot ticks.
Check your pets: Pets can also bring ticks indoors, so check them regularly and consider using tick prevention products recommended by your veterinarian.
Use tick repellent: Apply EPA-approved insect repellents containing DEET, picaridin, or permethrin to exposed skin and clothing. Permethrin can also be applied to clothing, gear, and footwear for added protection.
TICK REMOVAL
Do not panic! For Lyme disease, it can take up to 48 hours for the bacterium to transfer into the human body. So do tick checks often and try and remove them from your skin ASAP.
Use a pair of fine point tweezers to grip the tick as close to the skin as possible and pull straight out with steady pressure. You should not apply kerosene, petroleum jelly, nail polish, or a hot match tip to remove the tick. These measures are not effective and may result in injury.
Circle the calendar date and note where on the body the tick was removed. You may want to save the tick for identification.
Your physician may choose to treat you following a deer tick bite. Notify your health care provider if you have been bitten by a deer tick or if you develop a rash or other signs of illness following a tick bite such as fever, headache, fatigue, or sore and aching muscles.
