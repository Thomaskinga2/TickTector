2024 Tick-borne Disease Surveillance Summary
Introduction
The 2024 Tick-borne Disease Surveillance Summary provides data on infections reported to the Massachusetts Department of Public Health (DPH), Bureau of Infectious Disease and Laboratory Sciences by healthcare providers and laboratories. This report focuses on a subset of five tick-borne diseases: 
	•	Lyme disease
	•	Babesiosis
	•	Human granulocytic anaplasmosis (HGA)
	•	Borrelia miyamotoi infection 
	•	Powassan virus infection 

Lyme disease, babesiosis, anaplasmosis, Borrelia miyamotoi and Powassan virus are tick-borne diseases that are endemic to the Commonwealth. Lyme disease, babesiosis and anaplasmosis are common in Massachusetts while Borrelia miyamotoi and Powassan virus occur more rarely. Transmission of all five diseases can happen when an individual is bitten by a black-legged tick, also known as the deer tick (Ixodes scapularis).  Because these diseases are all transmitted by the same species of tick there is risk of co-infection with multiple pathogens from the same bite. Most infections occur in the warm spring and summer months when young (nymph) ticks are most active, though adult ticks may feed on humans and transmit disease year-round any time temperatures are above freezing. Black-legged ticks are most commonly found in grassy or wooded areas where deer and mice are present but are widely present in Massachusetts any place there is vegetation. 

For more information about these and other tick-borne diseases, please visit https://www.mass.gov/tick-borne-diseases.  This report includes age adjusted race and ethnicity values for Lyme, babesiosis, and anaplasmosis.  It should be noted that race/ethnicity data are often incompletely reported, which may affect the calculation and interpretation of rates presented in this summary, especially if data are missing in a nonrandom or biased way.

	•	Lyme disease is caused by the bacterium Borrelia burgdorferi. 
	•	8,842 probable cases of Lyme were reported in Massachusetts in 2024.
	•	Statewide, the Lyme incidence rate was 125.78 cases in 2024. The highest rate was seen in Dukes and Nantucket counties (754.55), with the next highest rate being in Berkshire County (439.45).
	•	The majority of cases developed symptoms between the months of June and August, but volume is high all year round. The time between exposure and disease onset for Lyme disease is between 3-30 days suggesting that most cases were exposed between May and August.
	•	Reported data show that people between the ages of 65 and 84 are at the greatest risk of contracting disease with the mean age for cases being 52 years old. However, historical information has demonstrated that children, especially males in the 5-14 year age range, also experience high rates of disease. Children are often diagnosed without laboratory testing and are therefore less likely to be included in surveillance data.
	•	Males are more likely than females to be infected, accounting for 58% of cases in the state.
	•	There were no known reported deaths in 2024.
	•	In January 2022, the national case definition for Lyme disease was revised so that Massachusetts and other high-incidence states report probable cases based on laboratory evidence alone, without need for additional clinical information.
	•	This change improves the ability of public health agencies to track and compare Lyme disease cases and trends in a standardized manner across high-incidence states moving forward and to use public health resources more efficiently.

Additional information about Lyme disease surveillance
The criteria used to define a case of Lyme disease for surveillance purposes has historically (since 1990) relied on collecting a combination of clinical information and laboratory testing results. While positive laboratory test results are almost entirely reported electronically and automatically to the DPH, obtaining clinical information relies on information that can only be provided by contacting the healthcare provider, and in some cases, the patient. Many states where Lyme disease is common, including Massachusetts, found that it was increasingly difficult to obtain sufficient clinical information to define a case as confirmed or probable. The challenges were a result of the high volume of suspect cases reported requiring investigation by public health departments and the large number of requests for clinical information made to healthcare providers. Over time, many “high incidence” states, those with the largest burden of Lyme disease cases, developed alternative surveillance strategies which reduced standardization of data across jurisdictions and reduced the utility of the collected data.

In 2016, the DPH stopped actively requesting clinical information for suspect cases reported with a positive laboratory test but continued to monitor laboratory tests and recorded clinical information from healthcare providers that chose to provide it. This strategy remained in place from 2016-2021 and resulted in a large number of suspect cases of Lyme disease (patients with any positive laboratory test) and very few confirmed or probable cases (patients with specific combinations of laboratory test and clinical information).

In 2022, led by the DPH, a new set of criteria to define cases of Lyme disease went into effect nationally. These criteria rely exclusively on laboratory test results in states where Lyme disease is already known to be common (called high incidence states). This definition https://www.cste.org/resource/resmgr/ps/ps2021/21-ID-05_Lyme_Disease.pdf substantially reduces the burden on public health and healthcare providers and supports greater use of resources on patient care and public health prevention messaging.

Babesiosis
	•	Babesiosis is a disease caused by a microscopic parasite (Babesia microti) that infects red blood cells.
	•	785 confirmed and probable cases of babesiosis were reported in Massachusetts in 2024.
	•	Statewide, babesiosis incidence slightly decreased from 11.9 in 2023 to 11.2 cases per 100,000 residents in 2024. The highest rate was seen in Dukes and Nantucket counties (94.7), which have historically seen the highest rates in the state. 
	•	The majority of cases developed symptoms between the months of June and August. The time between exposure and disease onset can be up to 6 weeks for babesiosis suggesting that most people were exposed between mid-April through July.
	•	The mean age for cases is 61 years old. Males are more likely than females to be infected, accounting for 61% of cases in the state.
	•	When asked about awareness of a recent tick bite prior to symptom onset, 17% of cases confirmed a known tick bite, 36.6% reported no known tick bite, while 18.3% were not sure (28% not reported).
	•	Individuals between the ages of 65 and 84 years of age are at greatest risk of contracting the disease.
	•	The most commonly reported symptoms included: fatigue (87%), fever (64%), headache (60%), joint pain (59%), and chills (53%); 30.4% of cases were hospitalized and there was one reported death.
	•	The primary source of transmission is via tick-bite; however, because it infects red blood cells, Babesia can also be transmitted through blood transfusions and organ donations. In 2024, there were three confirmed cases who received a blood transfusion in the six months prior to becoming ill. None of these cases were confirmed to have been exposed through blood transfusion.

Human Granulocytic Anaplasmosis (HGA)
	•	Anaplasmosis is a disease caused by the bacterium Anaplasma phagocytophilum.
	•	1,059 confirmed and probable cases of HGA were reported in Massachusetts in 2024.
	•	Statewide, HGA incidence increased from 10.3 in 2023 to 15.1 cases per 100,000 residents in 2024. The highest incidence rate was seen in Berkshire County (88.4), followed by Franklin County (77.4).
	•	The majority of cases developed symptoms during May to July during the time in which nymphal black-legged ticks are most active. The time between exposure and disease onset is about 2 weeks suggesting that exposures occurred between mid-April and July.
	•	When asked about awareness of a recent tick bite prior to symptom onset 30% of cases confirmed a known tick bite, 23% reported no known tick bite, while 16.3% were not sure (30.7% not reported).
	•	Individuals from ages 55 to 79 are at greatest risk of contracting the disease with the mean age for cases being 63 years old. Males were more likely than females to be infected, accounting for 61% of cases in the state.
	•	The most commonly reported symptoms included: malaise (82%), fever (78%), muscle pain (68%), headache (59%), and joint pain (57%), 27.3% of cases were hospitalized and there was one reported death.

Borrelia miyamotoi infection
	•	Borrelia miyamotoi is a type of spiral-shaped bacteria that is closely related to the bacteria that cause tick-borne relapsing fever. Infection is often referred to as borreliosis.
	•	B. miyamotoi is a relatively new, emerging disease, with the first recorded cases identified in New England in 2011. 
	•	57 confirmed and probable cases of Borrelia miyamotoi were reported in 2024. 
	•	The mean age of the cases was 61 years old. Males and females are nearly equally affected, with 53% of females and 47% of males being infected.  
	•	The majority of cases developed symptoms June through August with a smaller peak September through November. The incubation period for borreliosis is usually around 2 weeks
	•	Race data are not displayed for this disease due to the Bureau of Infectious Disease and Laboratory Sciences (BIDLS) data standards and suppression policies which are designed to protect patient’s identity. In this instance, the race data was both under the suppression threshold for most race groups and largely incomplete. 
	•	When asked about awareness of a recent tick bite prior to symptom onset 33% of cases confirmed a known tick bite, 40% reported no known tick bite, while 18% were not sure (9% not reported).
	•	The most commonly reported symptoms included: fatigue (88%), chills (82%), fever (81%), muscle pain (77%), headache (71%), and joint pain (68%); 19% of cases were hospitalized and there were no reported deaths.

Powassan virus 
	•	There are two types of Powassan virus in Massachusetts. Lineage one is found in ticks that feed on woodchucks (groundhogs); while lineage two (also known as Deer Tick virus) is carried by black-legged ticks. Powassan virus lineage 2 is the type most likely to be associated with human infections in Massachusetts.
	•	Most Powassan virus infection cases have been confirmed in Massachusetts via laboratory testing since 2013. 
	•	Between 2015 and 2024, 67 cases of Powassan virus infection were detected in Massachusetts residents.
	•	The mean age of those affected is 62, and majority male at 67%. 
	•	Race data are not displayed for this disease due to the BIDLS data standards and suppression rules. In this instance, the race data was both under the suppression threshold for most race groups and largely incomplete.
	•	Known tick bites before the onset of symptoms were reported in 25 out of 67 cases (37%), two cases (3%) reported no known tick bite, and 20 cases (30%) reported not knowing. Data were missing for 20 cases (30%). 
	•	The most common symptoms reported were fever (94%), change in mental status (82%), headache (79%), mental confusion (75%), muscle weakness (75%) and fatigue (69%).
	•	The primary types of infection reported were encephalitis (42%), meningoencephalitis (37%), and meningitis (12%).
	•	Most cases (93%) required hospitalization due to the severity of their symptoms, with 21 individuals (31%) being hospitalized more than once. 
	•	There have been 13 reported deaths.
